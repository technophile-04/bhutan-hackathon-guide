01· problem statement

credential verification.

Bhutan’s academic credential verification and equivalency process, especially for students studying in India and other countries abroad.

The current system for verifying and assessing international academic qualifications in Bhutan is characterized by inefficiencies, inconsistencies, and limited transparency, which collectively hinder effective recruitment processes. At present, the verification of credentials relies heavily on manual review, making the process time-consuming and prone to delays. This not only slows down decision-making but also places a significant administrative burden on reviewing authorities.

Furthermore, the absence of standardized guidelines often leads to inconsistent equivalency decisions. Different reviewers may interpret the same qualifications in varying ways, resulting in disparities that raise concerns about fairness and credibility. Compounding this issue is the growing challenge of verifying the authenticity of academic credentials, as there is no robust mechanism to directly validate documents with issuing institutions, increasing the risk of fraudulent or altered submissions.

The problem is further exacerbated by the lack of a centralized data system that consolidates information on recognized international institutions, accreditation bodies, and past equivalency decisions. Without such a unified platform, decision-making becomes fragmented and repetitive, reducing both efficiency and institutional memory. In addition, the current system offers limited transparency to applicants, who often receive little or no explanation regarding the acceptance or rejection of their qualifications, or the criteria used in determining equivalency.

Given these challenges, there is a critical need to rethink and modernize the system. This raises an important question: how might we design an intelligent, scalable, and blockchain-enabled solution that can automate the verification, interpretation, and conversion of international academic credentials into Bhutanese equivalency standards, while ensuring fairness, accuracy, transparency, and efficiency in the overall recruitment process.

02· problem statement

asset inventory.

Government asset inventory management using blockchains and Smart Contract for public procurement.

Every year, government agencies procure and manage thousands of assets—from IT equipment to infrastructure components—recorded in centralized database systems. On paper, these systems are structured and efficient. In practice, they tell a different story.

Records can be edited, overwritten, or deleted. Changes are not always visible, and audit trails are often incomplete or difficult to verify. When an asset goes missing, is reassigned, or its value changes, tracing the “true history” becomes a manual and time-consuming exercise. Procurement officers rely on data they cannot fully validate, auditors spend weeks reconciling inconsistencies, and decision-makers operate with limited confidence in the numbers presented to them.

As procurement cycles grow more complex and involve multiple agencies, the gaps widen—creating opportunities for errors, inefficiencies, and in worst cases, misuse of public resources. The system functions, but it does not inspire trust.

How might we transform government asset inventory and public procurement systems—currently reliant on mutable RDBMS records without a verifiable ledger—into a transparent, tamper-resistant, and trustworthy process using blockchain and smart contracts, so that procurement officers, auditors, and oversight bodies can reliably track, verify, and manage assets across their entire lifecycle?

03· problem statement

age verification.

Verifying the legal age for the sale of alcohol.

Alcohol consumption among youth is emerging as a growing public health concern in many parts of the world, including Bhutan. In Bhutan, national-level research indicates that alcohol use is widespread, with approximately 30.6% of adults reporting current alcohol use, and alcohol-related harm ranked among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in health facilities.

More concerningly, adolescent exposure is also notable. A school-based study reported that around 24% of Bhutanese students below 18 years of age were current alcohol users, highlighting early initiation of drinking behaviour among youth. At the same time, Bhutan enforces a legal minimum drinking age of 18 years, and vendors are required to verify age before selling alcohol. However, in practice, enforcement of age verification remains inconsistent, particularly in small retail shops, bars, and informal sales environments. This creates gaps where underage individuals may still be able to purchase alcohol due to weak identity checks, lack of reliable verification systems, or reliance on subjective judgment by shopkeepers.

Despite existing regulations, how might Bhutan ensure effective, reliable, and scalable verification of legal drinking age at the point of alcohol sale, while reducing underage access to alcohol and supporting shopkeepers in compliance?

04· problem statement

national drug issue.

National Drug Issue.

Substance abuse patterns that once appeared localised and intermittent in Bhutan have become increasingly complex over the past decade. The current drug landscape now includes illicit narcotics, diverted prescription drugs, and inhalant use among young people. Recent enforcement data indicate a steady rise in narcotics-related arrests and seizures, while health and social-service agencies report increasing demand for treatment, higher school absenteeism, and growing concern among communities.

Apprehensions for heroin abuse and/or trafficking increased from one case in 2023 to 28 cases in 2024. In the first five months of 2025 alone, the Royal Bhutan Police seized approximately 29 kilograms of heroin—exceeding the total quantity seized over the previous five years combined, although this was largely attributed to a single intercepted shipment. Global evidence suggests that heroin and emerging synthetic opioids, while still representing a smaller proportion of total drug cases in Bhutan, pose a disproportionately high risk of dependence, relapse, and overdose.

In 2025, more than 3,500 individuals were arrested in relation to drug-related offences, including substance abuse. Data further indicate that a significant proportion of those apprehended were youth under the age of 18. This trend poses a serious long-term risk to Bhutan’s human capital, particularly the productivity of its future workforce. Left unaddressed, substance abuse could weaken labour force participation, reduce productivity, and increase the burden on health, social, and justice systems.

Despite the growing magnitude of the problem, Bhutan currently lacks comprehensive empirical data on the scale, trends, and socio-economic impact of substance abuse. The last National Drug Use Survey was conducted in 2016, and its findings are now largely outdated and insufficient to inform contemporary policy decisions. At present, data collection across agencies remains fragmented and inconsistent, limiting the ability of policymakers to assess the full scope of the problem or design evidence-based interventions.

Strengthening national data systems and research capacity on drugs and substance abuse is therefore essential to support informed policy decisions, effective prevention strategies, and targeted treatment and rehabilitation services.

05· problem statement

the last mile.

Deliver/Reach to the Right Person, at the Right Place – Even When Addresses & Contacts Change

Background (The Real Pain)

Today, many critical deliveries fail not because of bad roads, but because of unreliable location and contact information:

  • A legal notice must be delivered to a specific flat number, but the building’s flat numbering system changed last year.
  • An insurance company needs to send a policy document to the right owner, but the owner shifted to another floor or building without updating records.
  • Police receive a fire/accident call, but the landmark description is vague (“near the old water tank”).
  • Ambulance pilots cannot find an emergency patient because the person changed their phone number and moved last week.
  • eCommerce agents waste hours trying to deliver parcels to “Flat 203” when the actual flat was renumbered to “2B” by the housing society.

The core problem is: People, buildings, flat numbers, and contact details change frequently.

06· problem statement

ordered signing.

In many organizations today, getting a document signed especially in a specific order is a slow, insecure, and hard-to-verify process.

Traditional signature solutions or thumb stamp marks often rely on manual or wet-ink signatures which can be forged, tampered with, or misused or take a long time to obtain. Worse, when disputes arise months or years later, proving that a document is authentic and unaltered is often difficult especially if signatures aren’t traceably bound to real identities. Moreover, it is difficult to make references for the legacy documents if necessary.

At the same time, modern signature workflows must satisfy two critical needs:

Human-Readable Signature

Signers and recipients expect to see a clear, visual signature like a “wet-ink” mark placed visibly on the document.

Cryptographic Non-Repudiation

Behind the scenes, every signature must also carry cryptographic proof—ensuring respective signers or an authorized person can verify who signed, in what order, and whether the file has been changed.

07· problem statement

one-touch service.

One Touch Public Service – Secure, Cardless, App-light Access for All Citizens

Background (The Real Pain)

In many regions, public service delivery is inefficient and excludes a large segment of users:

  • A literate citizen may know a service exists but struggles to navigate multiple portals, remember passwords, or carry physical cards (CID card, health ID, etc.).
  • An illiterate person can use a mobile phone (e.g., make calls, see icons) but cannot read forms, menus, or error messages. They don’t know which service to apply for or how to start.
  • Both groups fear fraud and identity theft - they don’t know if their data is safe or who accessed it.
  • Service providers (e.g., village officials, call center, govt officials) often help, but their actions are not traceable – no accountability if they misuse data or fail to deliver.

Worse, existing systems require:

  • Physical cards (lost, stolen, forged)
  • Smartphone apps (complicated, high storage, frequent updates)
  • OTPs / passwords (illiterates struggle; literate users get phishing attacks)

The core problem:

How can any citizen – whether they read or not – securely access any public service without cards or complex apps, using only their body (biometrics), while keeping their data tamper-proof and helper actions fully traceable?
